What universal design is

Universal design started with buildings and products before designers applied it to websites. This page explains the idea and its seven principles.

A definition

Ron Mace was an architect who used a wheelchair. He founded the Center for Universal Design at North Carolina State University, which defines universal design like this:

The design of products and environments to be usable by all people, to the greatest extent possible, without the need for adaptation or specialized design.

Common examples include ramps, lever door handles, automatic doors, and TV captions.

The seven principles

In 1997, a group of architects, product designers, engineers, and environmental design researchers at NC State wrote seven principles of universal design. Each one applies to websites too.

The seven principles of universal design, with web examples
Principle What it means On the web
Equitable use Everyone can use the same design. One site for every user instead of a separate accessible version
Flexibility in use People can use it in different ways. Every feature works with a mouse, keyboard, touch screen, or voice control
Simple and intuitive use It is easy to understand at any level of experience or language skill. Plain language and the same menu on every page
Perceptible information It presents information in more than one way. Alt text for images and captions for video
Tolerance for error It prevents mistakes and makes them easy to fix. Form error messages that say what went wrong and how to fix it
Low physical effort It takes little effort or strength to use. Fewer steps and autocomplete in forms
Size and space for approach and use It fits people of any size, posture, or mobility. Large buttons and links with space between them

Related terms

Accessibility
How well people with disabilities can perceive, understand, navigate, and interact with a site. WCAG is the standard used to measure it.
Universal design
One design that works for as many people as possible from the start.
Inclusive design
A design process that involves people who are often left out. Microsoft's Inclusive Design guide teaches this process.
Usability
How easy and efficient a site is to use.

Who we design for

  • People who are blind, have low vision, or are color blind
  • People who are deaf or hard of hearing
  • People with limited hand movement or tremors
  • People with cognitive or learning disabilities, like dyslexia
  • People with speech disabilities
  • Older adults and people reading in a second language
  • People on small screens, slow connections, or in bright sunlight

Assistive technology

Many people use assistive technology to read or control a page.

  • Screen readers like VoiceOver, NVDA, JAWS, and TalkBack read the page out loud.
  • Screen magnifiers and browser zoom make text and images bigger.
  • Switch devices and keyboards replace the mouse.
  • Voice control lets people click and type by speaking.
  • Refreshable braille displays show text as braille.

Norman's design concepts

Don Norman's book The Design of Everyday Things gives designers terms for how people understand objects. A signifier shows how to use an object, like a button that looks pressable. Feedback tells people that an action worked, like a message after someone submits a form.

Screen reader users do not see visual signifiers. The code has to describe each control instead, so a screen reader announces a real button element as a button.

WCAG and POUR

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, or WCAG, come from the W3C's Web Accessibility Initiative. The current version is WCAG 2.2, and its rules fall under four principles, called POUR:

Perceivable
Users can get the content through sight, hearing, or touch.
Operable
Users can use every control, with or without a mouse.
Understandable
Content is clear, and controls work the way users expect.
Robust
The code works with different browsers and assistive technology.

Each rule has a level of A, AA, or AAA. Most laws, including the ADA rule on the Why page, require Level AA.

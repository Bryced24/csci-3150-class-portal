Why universal design matters

Most websites still have accessibility errors that stop people from using them. This page covers who those errors affect and why designers should fix them.

Who uses the web

More than 1 in 4 adults in the United States have some type of disability, according to the CDC. Worldwide, the WHO estimates that 1.3 billion people have a significant disability. That is about 1 in 6 people.

The population is also getting older. U.S. Census projections show the share of people age 65 and older growing from about 18.7% in 2025 to 22% in 2040. Vision, hearing, movement, and memory can all change with age.

Permanent, temporary, and situational

Microsoft's Inclusive Design guide says people get left out when there is a mismatch between them and a design. That mismatch can be permanent, temporary, or situational.

Someone with one arm, someone with a broken arm, and a parent holding a baby all need to use a phone with one hand. A site that works with one hand helps all of them.

The curb cut effect

Curb cuts are the small ramps where a sidewalk meets the street. Disability activists in Berkeley, California, pushed the city to build them, and Berkeley installed its first official curb cut in 1972. Now people with strollers, bikes, carts, and suitcases use them too.

Web accessibility features work the same way. Captions also help people watching a video in a loud room, and keyboard shortcuts help people who do not want to reach for a mouse.

Most sites still fail

In February 2026, WebAIM tested the home pages of the top one million websites. Automated tests found accessibility errors on 95.9% of those pages, with an average of 56.1 errors per page.

The six most common errors have stayed the same for seven years. The How page shows how to fix each one.

The law

Section 508 requires federal agencies to make their websites accessible. In 2024, the Department of Justice added a rule under Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act that requires state and local governments to meet WCAG 2.1 Level AA. The rule covers public universities like CU Boulder, and their deadline is now April 26, 2027.

The European Accessibility Act has applied to many products and services since June 28, 2025.

Business and search

About 1 in 6 people worldwide have a significant disability, so an inaccessible site loses a large group of possible customers. Search engines also read alt text and headings, which makes accessible pages easier to find.

A grassroots movement

Designers have changed the web before. In 2003, CSS Zen Garden showed that CSS alone could give one HTML page many different designs. The Web Standards Project pushed browser makers to follow W3C standards, and HTML5 added semantic elements like header, nav, article, and footer.

Universal design is the next step. Tim Berners-Lee, who invented the web, put it this way:

The power of the Web is in its universality. Access by everyone regardless of disability is an essential aspect.
